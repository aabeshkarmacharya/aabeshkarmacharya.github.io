The Gap at Twice the Earth

The catalogue of known planets has a hole in it. Almost nothing is about 1.8 times the size of Earth. The hole is not a gap in what our telescopes can see — it is a gap in what is out there, and it was probably burned open by starlight.

The catalogue

Six thousand planets, unevenly found

The archive lists confirmed planets around stars. Plotted against their orbital periods and sizes, they do not fill the plane. They cluster into islands.

Most of those islands are us, not them. Two methods have found almost everything: a planet crossing its star dims it by a part in ten thousand, and a planet tugging its star shifts the starlight’s colour by a few metres per second. Both work best on big planets in tight orbits, so that is what the catalogue is mostly made of. The empty bottom-right of the plot — small planets on wide orbits — is not a statement about the universe. It is the shape of our instruments, printed onto the sky.

There is a subtler trap in the same picture, and it is worth getting out of the way first, because the rest of this essay depends on avoiding it. The archive’s composite table fills its own gaps. A planet found by the wobble method has a mass but no measured size, so the archive predicts a size from the mass and files it in the same column as the measured ones. Of the planets with both a period and a radius here, only have a radius that was actually observed. The other are the output of a formula. Drawn together without comment, they would put a crowd of planets on the plot at sizes nobody ever measured.

The measurement

A hole at 1.8 Earth radii

Ignore the wide orbits, where we are blind, and look only at the close ones, where we are not. Count planets by size. The count does not fall away smoothly from small to large. It has two humps and a notch between them.

A planet’s radius is a ratio. A transit gives the size of the planet relative to its star, so a planet’s size is only as good as the star’s, and for much of the catalogue the star is a stranger. An uncertainty of twenty per cent will smear a thirty per cent notch into nothing at all. So the sample is cut on precision rather than on size: transiting planets whose radius is known to better than eight per cent, orbiting closer than days, around unevolved stars between 3,900 and 6,500 kelvin. That leaves planets.

The notch sits at Earth radii, between a super-Earth peak at and a sub-Neptune peak at . At the bottom of it, planets are less common than the two peaks either side. Resampling the catalogue a thousand times moves the notch only between and Earth radii, and in of those thousand draws it is still there. This feature has a name: the Fulton gap, after the survey that pinned it down in 2017.

The controls below are not decoration. A result that exists at one setting of four knobs is not a result, so all four move. Loosen the precision cut to twenty per cent and the sample doubles while the gap shallows from to about a quarter — that is measurement error filling it in, not planets appearing. Widen the smoothing and the same thing happens more gently, until at about 0.055 the two peaks merge into one bump and the finder gives up and says so, which is the honest failure mode of asking a blurry picture a sharp question. Tighten the precision cut too far instead and it fails the other way, for the opposite reason: barely two hundred planets survive, and two hundred planets cannot resolve anything.

The objection

Could we just be missing them?

A transit survey finds big planets more easily than small ones. If sensitivity fell off a cliff at 1.8 Earth radii, a gap would appear there whether or not the planets did. This is the objection the measurement has to survive, and it can be answered with the survey’s own numbers rather than a model of it.

Kepler’s pipeline recorded, for every candidate it flagged, the signal-to-noise of the dimming it found. Read that off as a function of planet size and the answer is unambiguous: detectability climbs smoothly and monotonically straight through the gap. The transit depth roughly trebles across the range shown, in even steps, with no feature of any kind at 1.8 Earth radii. Nothing about the telescope, the pipeline or the noise changes there.

That is the whole argument, and it is a strong one, because a smooth sensitivity curve cannot carve a local minimum into a smooth underlying distribution. Sensitivity that falls with size can suppress the small end — and it does; that is why the count drops away below one Earth radius. It cannot dig a pit in the middle and leave both sides standing.

There is a second check sitting in the same table, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not show. Rebuild the size distribution from Kepler’s own pipeline — its stellar parameters, its radii, its candidates alongside the confirmed planets — and the notch is still there, at Earth radii and deep. Most of those objects are the same planets as before, so this is not an independent sample. It is an independent measurement chain: different stellar radii, chosen by a pipeline rather than by the archive’s editors picking between published papers. The gap does not care which of them you trust.

The candidates that were never confirmed would make a genuinely independent sample, and it is tempting to use them. There are too few: after the same precision cut, barely a hundred survive, and the gap-finder run on them returns a different answer every time it is resampled. That is a sample too small to say anything with, which is a more useful result than a number would have been.

What they are made of

Rock on one side, sky on the other

Size alone would only be a curiosity. What makes the gap mean something is that the planets on either side of it are made of different stuff, and you can tell by weighing them.

This is where the archive’s helpfulness has to be refused outright. Of its planetary masses, — nearly half — were not measured but computed from the radius through a mass–radius relation. Plot those against radius and you recover the relation you put in, a perfect curve with no scatter, and it means precisely nothing. Keeping only genuinely weighed planets, with a mass good to 25% and a radius good to 16%, leaves of them.

The split is stark. Below the gap, the median density is grams per cubic centimetre — denser than Earth, which is 5.51, and consistent with bare rock and iron. Above the gap it is , far too light for rock at any composition: of them sit above the pure-rock line, meaning they are too large for their mass unless they are wrapped in something very light indeed.

How light can be pinned down further. Water ice is the lightest thing a planet can be built from in bulk, and an ocean world of a given mass is about a third larger than a rocky one. Yet of these planets are larger still — too big for their mass even if they were made of nothing but water. Whatever is holding them up cannot be rock, iron or ice. It has to be gas. A hydrogen envelope of one or two per cent by mass is enough, because hydrogen at those temperatures is enormously puffy. The sub-Neptunes are not bigger rocks. They are the same rocks wearing an atmosphere.

The explanation

Starlight takes the atmosphere

If the sub-Neptunes are rocky cores in hydrogen coats, the gap is the place where the coat comes off. A young star floods its close planets with X-rays and ultraviolet; the light heats the top of a thin hydrogen envelope past escape velocity, and the atmosphere boils away. A planet that keeps its envelope stays large. One that loses it collapses to its bare core and turns up on the other side of the gap. Very few are caught mid-undress, so the middle stays empty.

That story makes a prediction sharp enough to fail. Stripping is easier closer in, so the largest planet that can be stripped should be larger close to the star than far from it. The gap should therefore tilt: down with orbital period, up with the starlight received. A rival account — that these planets simply formed late, in gas-poor disks, and never had much atmosphere to lose — predicts the tilt in the opposite direction.

Fitting a straight line to the emptiest path through the plane, using all planets at once rather than a handful of binned minima, the gap runs as R ∝ P against orbital period and R ∝ S against starlight received. Photoevaporation predicts −0.15 and +0.11; gas-poor formation predicts +0.11 and a downward tilt in starlight. The measurement has the signs the mass-loss picture requires, in both variables, and of a thousand bootstrap fits keep the period slope negative.

This distinguishes mass loss from late formation. It does not separate the two mass-loss mechanisms from each other: photoevaporation by the young star and cooling-driven escape powered by the planet’s own formation heat predict tilts too similar to tell apart with a thousand planets. The honest statement is that something strips these atmospheres, and the catalogue cannot yet say what heats them.

How we got here

Thirty-four years

None of this was visible before about 2014. The gap is a statistical feature of a large, uniformly measured sample, and there was no such sample until one telescope stared at one patch of sky for four years.

The first confirmed planets around a normal star arrived in , one at a time, by the wobble method. Kepler changed the units: its planets came in validated batches of hundreds, which is why the chart below has spikes rather than a trend. TESS now supplies the steady flow, and because it surveys the whole sky rather than one field, its planets orbit brighter, nearer stars whose radii are known well — which is what a sharper version of this measurement will need.

What this does not show

Four limits

The sample is not complete. Nothing here is corrected for detection efficiency. The gap survives that omission because the argument turns on the shape of the sensitivity curve rather than its height, but the relative size of the two peaks is not a measurement of how common the two kinds of planet are.

The stars are strangers. Radii here come from many surveys with many methods of characterising the host. The published sharpest versions of this measurement use one spectroscopic survey of one sample; this one buys breadth at the cost of a blurrier gap. That the gap survives the blur is a point in its favour, not a claim to have measured it best.

The gap is not empty. It is a deficit of about , not a void. Planets do live there.
