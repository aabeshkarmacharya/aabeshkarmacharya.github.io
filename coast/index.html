How fast is the sea coming?

The global figure is the wrong number. A tide gauge is bolted to a pier and measures the distance down to the water, so it records the ocean rising and the ground sinking together — and it is the sum that floods a street. At one coast in five that sum is negative and the sea is going away. Pick a gauge and this says what its own record actually shows.

What a straight line through a pier cannot tell you

This is relative sea level, and that is the point. The gauge cannot separate the ocean rising from the land sinking, and for anyone standing on that coast it does not need to: the sum is what arrives at the door. It does mean the number is about this pier. Carrying it to a coast fifty kilometres away with different geology can be badly wrong, which is what the neighbours table above is for.

Projecting a straight line is a floor, not a forecast. The arithmetic above extends the observed trend unchanged. Three gauges in four are already steeper over their last thirty years than over their whole record, so a line fitted to the whole record is, for most coasts, known to be too shallow. Both numbers are shown for exactly that reason, and the steeper one is still not a projection of the future — it is a projection of the recent past.

The average is not what floods you. Damage arrives when a surge and a high tide land on top of the mean. Raising the mean by a few centimetres does not sound like much and is not meant to: it works by moving every extreme up with it, so the level that used to arrive once a century starts arriving far more often.

The archive is not evenly spread. Long records are concentrated in Europe, North America and Japan; much of Africa, South America and the Pacific has gauges that are young, interrupted, or absent. A coast with no dot on the map above is not a coast with no problem.
