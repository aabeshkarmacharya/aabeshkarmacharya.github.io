Twelve Hundred Metres Lower

On Nepal’s peaks, the people who are paid to climb and the people who pay to climb now die at almost the same rate. They do not die the same deaths, in the same places, for the same reasons — and that difference has not narrowed at all.

Two populations

Everyone on the mountain, counted twice

Elizabeth Hawley spent fifty years in Kathmandu interviewing climbers as they came down. The archive she built records every expedition to every Nepali peak since 1905 — of them, and individual people.

It also does something few records of dangerous work do: it counts the workers. Each person on an expedition is marked as a member, who paid to be there, or as hired staff, who were paid to be there. Of the hired appearances in the archive, are Nepali citizens. So this is a rare thing: a complete employment record of one of the most dangerous jobs in the world, kept for a century.

Hired is a job, not an ethnicity. The word “Sherpa” names a people of the Solukhumbu, and it is used loosely in mountaineering writing to mean anyone Nepali carrying a load. This page uses the archive’s own distinction, which is employment, and says “hired staff” throughout. The two are not the same: Nepalis appear in these records as members, leaders and climbers rather than as staff.

How often

The gap that closed

Start with the obvious question, because the obvious answer turns out to be wrong. Across the whole archive, hired staff die at per thousand against for members — half again as often. Split that by decade and nearly all of it turns out to be history.

On Everest alone the ordering actually reverses — members , hired — though there too the intervals overlap and the honest reading is that they are indistinguishable.

Some of that convergence is real. The route through the Khumbu Icefall is now built and maintained by a dedicated team rather than by each expedition in turn; fixed rope runs continuously on the commercial peaks; a helicopter can reach base camp. Equipment that used to be the client’s privilege is now standard for staff.

But some of it is the archive changing rather than the mountain. In the 1970s the record contains hired appearances against member ones — one worker for every ten clients, which is not how those expeditions were staffed. Porters and low-altitude workers went largely unrecorded; the ones who died were far more likely to be written down than the ones who walked home. A record that captures deaths more completely than it captures people will report a death rate that is too high, and that is almost certainly part of what the 1970s figure of per thousand is. By the 2000s hired staff make up about a fifth of every recorded party and by the 2010s a third, which is what the modern commercial mountain actually looks like.

How

The mountain falls on one group and wears down the other

This is the finding that does not converge. Sort the deaths by cause and the two populations separate almost completely, and they have stayed separated through every era the archive covers.

Count the deaths caused by something falling — avalanche, icefall collapse, rock and ice, a crevasse opening underneath. of hired deaths since 2000 are of that kind, against of member deaths. Members are far more often killed by a fall, by altitude sickness, by exhaustion or by cold: the mountain acting on a body over hours and days, in a place where the body is already failing.

That distinction is not about skill or courage, and it is not really about luck. It is about what the job is. Objective hazard — the kind that kills you regardless of how well you are climbing — is a property of ground, and you are exposed to it in proportion to how often you cross that ground. A client crosses the Khumbu Icefall perhaps four to six times in a season. A worker ferrying loads and fixing rope crosses it many times more. Neither of them is any safer per crossing. One of them crosses far more often.

Where

Twelve hundred metres

Every death in the archive has a height. Plot them and the whole argument collapses into one picture: the two groups die on different parts of the mountain.

Since 2000 the median member death is at metres — high on the upper mountain, near where the climbing is hardest and the air is thinnest. The median hired death is at metres. That is not the summit ridge. On Everest it is the Khumbu Icefall and the ground just above it: the moving part of the mountain that everything and everyone has to be carried through, over and over, before any climbing begins.

The workers die metres below the clients. They are killed on the commute.

How they arrive

One at a time, or sixteen at once

There is a second consequence of being killed by falling ground rather than by altitude, and it does not show up in any average. A fall kills one person. An avalanche kills everyone standing underneath it.

Since 2000, of hired deaths came in expeditions that lost three or more people at once, against of member deaths. The worst single expedition in the record lost hired staff. Whole years are shaped by single mornings: in the archive records hired deaths, most of them from one serac collapse in the icefall above base camp; the following year an earthquake brought part of Pumori down onto Everest base camp itself.

This matters beyond arithmetic. A risk that arrives in clusters falls on a community rather than on individuals: the 2014 icefall took men from a handful of villages in the Khumbu, several of them from the same families. An average death rate of nine in a thousand describes none of that.

Which mountain

It depends enormously on the peak

Everest dominates the commercial record and is not representative of it. On the peaks with enough traffic to measure, the two groups’ rates diverge in both directions.

The pattern that emerges is not “hired staff are always at greater risk”. It is that the two groups are exposed to different mountains even when they are standing on the same one. Where the danger is concentrated in the approach and the load-carrying — steep, avalanche-prone ground low down — the staff bear it. Where the danger is concentrated in the summit push and the descent, the clients do. Everest, with a long dangerous approach and a very long summit day, manages to be both, which is why its two rates land on top of each other.

These are small numbers. A peak with four hundred hired appearances and six deaths gives a rate that moves by more than two per thousand every time one more person dies, which is why every bar carries its interval and why peaks with fewer than a hundred people in either group are not shown at all.

The industry

A third of the mountain is now staff

The reason any of this is measurable is that the work became an industry. In the 1970s the archive records roughly one hired appearance for every ten members. By the 2010s it is one in three.

The same period turned the hired role from portering into guiding. Since 2000, hired staff reach the summit of the time against for members, and use supplementary oxygen more often, not less ( against ). They are also younger: a median of against . The people most likely to stand on the summit of Everest in a given season are the people being paid to be there.

What this does not show

Four limits

The exposure is not equal, and cannot be corrected here. Every rate on this page is per person per expedition. The archive does not record how many times anyone crossed the icefall, so the fact that a worker crosses it several times more often than a client is stated and never quantified. Equal deaths per season, with unequal exposure, means the ground is safer per crossing for nobody and the job is more dangerous for one of them.

A career is not a season. A worker who returns for fifteen seasons faces the seasonal risk fifteen times; a client who comes once faces it once. Nothing here is a lifetime risk, and the lifetime risks of the two groups are not remotely comparable.

The early record under-counts the living. Deaths are recorded far more reliably than routine participation, and hired staff were the ones most often left out. Rates before about 2000 are upper bounds, and the long decline is partly the archive improving.

It ends in 2019. The export used here closes after the spring 2019 season. The record crowds, the permit changes and every season since are outside it.
