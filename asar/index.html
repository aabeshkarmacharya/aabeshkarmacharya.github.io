One Rupee in Three, Spent in Asar

In the median year, Nepal’s government makes of its capital payments in Asar, the last month of the fiscal year. That is about ordinary months of spending in one. Since 2016 the budget has been due seven weeks before the year opens. Spending in the first half of the year has risen since then. The share left for Asar has barely moved.

Read this first

Asar is a subtraction

Nepal Rastra Bank’s monthly bulletin states what the government has spent so far in the fiscal year. The issue for eleven months and the annual issue come out a month apart. Subtract one from the other and what is left is Asar, mid-June to mid-July. Nothing else in the archive measures that month on its own.

This page reads the same bulletins as the remittance and interest-rate pages. of them are real PDFs with a text layer, and they cover months of fiscal years. of those months state the government’s capital spending in a form this page could read. The other print only total spending, or a percentage change with no level, or nothing on the budget at all. issues filed under the wrong month were moved to the month their own pages name.

NRB rewrote the sentence many times. In 2008 it was “the capital expenditure went up by 35.8 percent to Rs 28.2 billion”. In 2012 it was “stood at 40.83 billion”, with no currency at all. Since 2020 it has been one sentence that lists recurrent, capital and financial spending and then gives three numbers in that order. From 2015 to 2018 some issues also print a budget box whose columns the text layer runs together, as capital335176.060084.317.93: a budget, an outturn, and the outturn as a share of the budget. The parser tries every way of cutting such a string and keeps the one whose arithmetic works.

Later bulletins fill some of the holes. Every issue restates the same months of the year before, as a level or as a percentage change, and since 2021 a small table sets the last three years side by side. A figure is drawn solid when its own bulletin states it, outlined when a later bulletin prints it, and dotted when only a later percentage change implies it. Only solid figures go into the headline numbers.

The limitation that matters

The bulletins count payments, not work

Every figure here is on a cash basis. Spending is counted when the treasury pays it, so a road finished in Falgun and paid for in Asar is Asar spending.

So the Asar figure measures when money left the government. It cannot say when the work was done, or how well. Some of what is paid in Asar is work finished earlier and billed late. Some is work hurried to completion before the year’s budget lapses. The bulletins cannot tell the two apart, and this page does not try.

Two other things change underneath the series. From 2017/18, the first year of Nepal’s elected provincial and local governments, the bulletins report the federal government alone. What the provinces and municipalities spend, and when, is not in them. And the total a bulletin first prints for a year is not the last word on it, which the section after next takes up.

Read every number below as a statement about payments by the central government. That is a real quantity, and it is the one the budget controls. It is not a measure of construction, and the archive holds nothing that would turn it into one.

The finding

A third of the year’s capital spending, in one month

Take the years whose own bulletins give both figures. In the median one, of the year’s capital spending is paid in Asar. A year spent at an even pace would pay in each month, so Asar carries times its even share.

The curve shows where the year goes. By the end of Poush, half-way through, the median year has paid out of its capital spending. By the end of Jestha, with one month left, it has paid . The rest is Asar. In 2009 NRB listed the pattern itself among the reasons capital spending had grown:

The trend of increasing capital expenditure in the later months of the year, payment for the contracts, smoothness in public procurement processes … contributed to such a growth in the capital expenditure in the review year.

NRB, annual bulletin for 2008/09

Recurrent spending is the control. It is salaries, running costs and grants, paid on schedules that do not wait for a contract to be finished. It is not spread evenly either: of it lands in Asar in the median year, about times an even share. Capital spending is times as concentrated. By the end of Poush the median year has paid of its recurrent bill and of its capital one.

Nepali has a phrase for the pattern: asare bikas, Asar development. Asar is also the month the monsoon arrives. The Department of Hydrology and Meteorology puts the normal onset in eastern Nepal at 13 June, within days of the month’s first day.

What was budgeted

A third of the capital budget is never spent

For years between and the bulletins also state the capital budget each year began with. Against it, the median year had spent by the end of Jestha, spent another in Asar, and left unspent.

Each of those three is the median of its own column, so they need not add to a hundred. Each year’s bar in the figure does. The median year spent of its capital budget in all. After the bulletins stop stating the budget, so the figure stops too.

NRB sees the result in its own books. Money the government raises and does not spend sits in its account at the central bank:

The accumulation of cash balance of the government at NRB due to low expenditure relative to resource mobilization implies the existence of entrenched structural problems in the public spending system.

NRB, annual bulletin for 2014/15

What is not here

Five things the archive will not support

What was built. Stated once already, and it is the limit that matters most. These are payments. Nothing in the bulletins measures a road, a bridge or a school, or whether one finished in Asar was any good.

The provinces and municipalities. Since 2017/18 they spend part of the country’s budget, much of it received as grants from the federal government. When they spend it is not in these bulletins.

The last week. The Financial Comptroller General Office reports spending day by day, and that would show how much of Asar lands in its final days. The bulletins are monthly, so this page stops at the month.

Freeze accounts. Until 2012/13 the bulletins print a separate line, “expenditure from freeze accounts”, for money from the previous year’s budget spent early in the new one. It is not in the capital figures here, and most issues do not say how much of it was capital.
