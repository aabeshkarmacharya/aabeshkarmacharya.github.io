Structural assessment

Nothing on this page is about your building, and your building is the entire question. Two houses on the same street came through 2015 differently, and the difference was how they were built, not where they stood. An engineer who walks the structure can tell you which one yours is.

The record is too short to see Nepal's worst

This catalogue begins in 1976. Nepal's previous great earthquake was in 1934, four decades before it, and the one before that in the nineteenth century. Great Himalayan earthquakes recur on intervals longer than the whole instrumental record, so a page that counts 51 years is looking through a window too narrow to contain the event it most matters to see. Every rate below is therefore a floor, not an estimate, and the gap between the two is not something this page can measure.

It counts earthquakes; it does not model shaking. How hard the ground moves at a particular address depends on the depth and geometry of the rupture, the rock between, and above all the soil directly underneath — Kathmandu sits on a lake bed that amplifies motion. Turning a magnitude and a distance into a shaking figure needs all of that, and a wrong ground-motion model produces a confident number nobody can see is wrong. So none is used here.

These earthquakes are not independent. About 66% of the events in this archive are triggered by another event already in it — the neighbouring essay's own finding. A tole that records forty earthquakes may have had four sequences, not forty occasions. The share falls sharply with size: the largest events are mostly not anybody's aftershock.

Distance is to the epicentre, which is a point, and a large earthquake is not. The 2015 rupture ran some 150 km along the fault. A town measured 80 km from the epicentre may have sat directly above the slipping surface.
