Where the Sea Is Falling

About one tide gauge in five reports that the sea beside it is getting lower. They are not wrong, and they are not measuring a different ocean. They are measuring a different piece of land.

What a tide gauge measures

The instrument is bolted to the thing it is measuring against

A tide gauge sits on a pier and records how far down the water is. Over a century that record accumulates two quantities it cannot separate: how much the sea rose, and how much the pier moved.

The second term is not a rounding error. Ice sheets kilometres thick pressed down the crust beneath Scandinavia and Canada, and since they melted the land has been springing back — still, ten thousand years later, at up to a centimetre a year. River deltas compact under their own weight. Cities pumping groundwater from beneath themselves sink into the hole. And a subduction earthquake can drop a coastline half a metre between one tide and the next.

All of that arrives in the archive labelled as sea level. Fitting a straight line to each of the records long enough to support one gives trends from to millimetres a year — a spread of four centimetres a year between the extremes, around a median of . The median is roughly the number usually quoted for the ocean. Almost everything else in that range is land.

These numbers cannot be compared between stations, only within one. Each record is measured against a datum chosen for that gauge alone, usually set about seven metres below the readings to keep them positive. A station’s absolute value means nothing outside its own record. Its slope is the only portable quantity, and the slope is all this page uses.

The spread

There is no such thing as the local rate

Laid out as a distribution, the trends make an awkward shape for a number that gets quoted as a single figure. The bulk sits between and millimetres a year, with a long tail in each direction.

Being negative is not the same as being certainly negative, and the distinction matters when the noise is decadal. Of the records, fall and rise with 95% confidence, and the remainder cannot be told apart from no change at all. Those intervals are widened for the fact that sea level next month resembles sea level this month; treating a century of monthly readings as a century of independent measurements would make every one of these trends look about five times more certain than it is.

The evidence

The falling gauges are all in the same places

If the falling records were noise, they would be scattered. They are not. Sorted by latitude, they collect almost entirely into one band, and it is the band that was under ice.

Between 60 and 70 degrees north — the Gulf of Bothnia, the Baltic coast of Finland, Hudson Bay, southern Alaska — the median gauge reports the sea falling, and of them do. Every other ten-degree band on Earth has a rising median. This is the fingerprint of the Fennoscandian and Laurentide ice sheets, which stopped existing about ten thousand years ago and whose absence the crust is still adjusting to.

Four records

Half a metre of sea level in one afternoon

The clearest way to see that these instruments measure land is to watch the land move.

On 11 March 2011 the Pacific plate slipped beneath north-east Japan and the coastline above it dropped. The gauge at Onahama recorded the sea rising millimetres and staying there — about two and a half centuries of global mean sea level rise, arriving in an afternoon and never going away. recorded millimetres. The ocean did not rise. Japan fell.

The record does something else worth noticing afterwards. The level at Onahama drifts back down over the following decade — not because the sea is retreating, but because the crust is slowly recovering some of what it dropped. That post-seismic relaxation is visible in the annual means as a decade-long decline that has nothing whatever to do with the ocean.

Set against that, Brest has been keeping records since — the longest series in the world, on a stable piece of Brittany, and it climbs at a modest mm a year. And the gauge at the mouth of the Chao Phraya below Bangkok rises at — not because the Gulf of Thailand is behaving differently from the rest of the ocean, but because the city behind it has been pumping out the aquifer it stands on.

What is changing

Steeper than it was, nearly everywhere

Land motion of the slow kind — rebound, compaction — is close to constant over a century. So comparing a station against its own past is a way of subtracting most of the land and seeing what the ocean is doing.

Fitting the last thirty years of each record separately, of the stations with enough data are rising faster now than over their full record. The median station has gained millimetres a year, taking the median from over the whole record to in the last three decades.

That is the part of the signal the land cannot easily explain. A pier does not start sinking faster at three quarters of the stations at once. Note what this comparison costs, though: a thirty-year window is short enough that decadal ocean cycles leave a mark on it, so the size of the change is less certain than its direction.

The archive

In 1850 every gauge on Earth was in Europe

Everything above is bounded by one fact about the record: it is not a sample of the world’s coastlines. It is a sample of the coastlines that had somebody writing numbers down.

In 1850 the archive contains seven gauges, and all seven are European. By 1900 there are , of which are still European. The southern hemisphere never catches up: even today of recording gauges are north of the equator, on a planet whose southern half holds most of the ocean.

So a nineteenth-century global mean built from tide gauges is a north-west European mean wearing a global label. This does not undo the modern result — there are enough gauges now, spread widely enough — but it does mean the early part of any long sea-level curve is carrying far more of Europe than of Earth.

What this does not show

Four limits

The land is never subtracted. Separating vertical land motion from sea level needs GPS at the gauge or a glacial-rebound model, and neither is used here. Every trend on this page is land plus sea, which is the essay’s subject rather than its flaw — but it means no number here is a measurement of the ocean alone.

A straight line is a poor model of a century. Sea level is not linear and the residuals are strongly autocorrelated. The intervals are widened for that, crudely; they should be read as indicative rather than exact.

Some of these gauges are on rivers. The largest rising trend in the archive belongs to a station on the Hooghly, where the record carries river regime and dredging as well as sea and land. Gauges are not screened for this here.

Coverage is uneven in space and in time, as above. Nothing on this page is area-weighted, so the median gauge is not the median coastline and certainly not the median square kilometre of ocean.
