Eleven Years, Roughly

Every month since 1749, somebody has counted the spots on the sun. This is all of it — 3,332 numbers, a rhythm you can see from across the room, and not one cycle the same length as another.

SILSO, Royal Observatory of Belgium  
The record

The longest experiment nobody meant to run

Nobody set out to build a 277-year time series. Astronomers pointed telescopes at the sun, drew what they saw, and counted. Then someone else did it the next year, and the next, and eventually the counting outlived everyone who started it.

What came out is one of the longest continuously measured quantities in science, and almost the only direct record of the sun's behaviour that predates instruments capable of measuring anything else about it. Rudolf Wolf assembled it in the 1850s from the observations of people already dead, and it has been maintained without a gap ever since.

The pattern in the hero is not subtle. Neither is the fact that it is irregular: the cycles differ in height by a factor of three and a half, and in length by more than four years. Any account of the sun has to explain both the regularity and the failure of the regularity, and after 175 years of trying, nobody can predict the next cycle's size from the last one.

Finding the period

Eleven point one

The eleven-year cycle is usually stated rather than derived, which is a shame, because deriving it takes one line of arithmetic and the answer is more interesting than the round number.

Take the 3,332 monthly values, subtract the mean, apply a window, and Fourier transform them. The power spectrum has one dominant peak, and it sits at

11.107 years.

Not eleven. The autocorrelation agrees: the series is most similar to itself at a lag of 10.7 years, and most dissimilar at 5.25 years — half a cycle later, which is exactly where a periodic signal should be at its most out of step with itself.

The width of that peak is the more interesting number. A perfectly periodic signal observed for 277 years cannot produce an infinitely sharp peak — the finite record smears it to about 1.4 resolution elements. This peak is 3.0 across: 2.1 times broader than the record length alone can explain. Its full width at half maximum runs from 10.28 to 11.57 years.

That extra width is the sun failing to keep time, and it can be checked independently. Measuring the 24 cycles individually gives a mean length of 11.03 years with a standard deviation of 1.19. The spectrum says the period wanders by roughly a year; counting the cycles one at a time says the same thing. Two different methods, same answer, and neither of them is eleven.

Cycle to cycle

None of them are the same

Locating each minimum and maximum on the smoothed series gives 24 complete cycles between 1755 and 2020. Their lengths run from 9.00 years to 13.58. Their heights run from 81 to 285. Laying them on top of one another, aligned at their starting minima, shows how little the shape is fixed.

The one thing that does predict

Almost nothing about a cycle can be forecast from the cycle before it. But there is a relationship within each cycle, noticed by Max Waldmeier in 1935: the cycles that rise fastest are the ones that rise highest.

Correlating rise time against amplitude across all 24 cycles gives r = −0.749, on 24 points, t = −5.3. Cycles that take three years to reach maximum average well over 200. Cycles that take six years struggle past 120.

Two suns

The hemispheres do not agree

Since 1992 the counts have been kept separately for the northern and southern halves of the solar disc, and they turn out to be surprisingly independent. Across 416 months, the average imbalance between them is 36 per cent of the total. In 115 of those months — more than a quarter — one hemisphere produced more than twice as many spots as the other.

Over the whole period the two nearly balance, with the south ahead by about nine per cent. They arrive at that balance by taking turns, sometimes for years at a stretch.

Data honesty

Who was looking, and how hard

A sunspot number is not a measurement of the sun. It is a count of what an observer saw through a particular telescope on a particular day, scaled by a factor meant to make different observers comparable. That construction has consequences.

The daily series begins in 1818, and for its first four decades between a fifth and nearly two fifths of the days have no observation at all — 3,247 missing days in total. The gaps close completely in 1850 and never reopen.

The second column is stranger. The file records how many observations went into each day's value, and for every single day before 1981 that number is 1. The historical daily series is not an average of many observers; it is a reconstruction from one reference series, carried forward. Only from 1981 does the count climb — to a mean of 39 by the early 2020s, with a maximum of 69 observers on one day in August 2020.

The series has been rewritten once already. In 2015 the sunspot number was recalibrated end to end — the first revision since Wolf — after historical cross-checks showed a drift in the reference observer's scaling. Values before 1947 moved by roughly 45 per cent. Every figure on this page uses version 2.0, the post-revision series. Papers written before 2015 are describing different numbers for the same sun.

That is the same lesson the counts themselves keep teaching. The eleven-year cycle is a property of the sun. Almost everything else on this page is a property of the sun and the people watching it, mixed together in a proportion that has to be worked out rather than assumed.

Now

Where the current cycle sits

Cycle 25 began at the minimum of late 2019 and has been running for 81 months. Its smoothed maximum so far is 160.9, reached in late 2024.

That places it fifteenth of the twenty-five cycles since 1755 — comfortably above Cycle 24, the weakest in a century, and comfortably below the 179 average. It is also substantially above what most forecasts issued in 2019 predicted, which were clustered around a repeat of Cycle 24.

There is no trend here to extract. Twenty-five cycles is a small sample of something with structure on timescales longer than the record. The honest summary of 277 years of counting is that the sun has a rhythm of about eleven years, keeps it badly, and has so far given no indication of why.

Method & sources

All data from SILSO, the World Data Center for the Sunspot Index and Long-term Solar Observations at the Royal Observatory of Belgium, retrieved 5 September 2026: monthly means from 1749, daily values from 1818, yearly means from 1700, and hemispheric monthly means from 1992. Series version 2.0.

Cycle boundaries are the minima of the 13-month smoothed series, S[i] = (x[i−6]/2 + x[i−5] + … + x[i+5] + x[i+6]/2) / 12, located as the strict minimum of a ±4-year window. That reproduces the conventional numbering exactly, with Cycle 1 beginning at 1755.12 and Cycle 24 ending at 2019.96 — which is a reasonable check that the procedure is doing what the convention does.

The power spectrum is a Hann-windowed periodogram of the full monthly series with the mean removed; the peak sits at 11.107 years and is unchanged if the window is omitted. Peak width is quoted against the record's own resolution: 277.7 years of data gives a frequency resolution of 1/T = 0.0036 yr−1, a Hann window spreads a pure tone to about 1.4 of those, and the observed full width at half maximum is 0.0108 yr−1, or 3.0. The autocorrelation was computed on the same mean-removed series.

The Waldmeier correlation is Pearson's r on the 24 complete cycles, with rise time measured from smoothed minimum to smoothed maximum. Cycle 25 is excluded from every statistic that requires a completed cycle, and included only where it is labelled as in progress.

Every number quoted here was computed from the downloaded files. Where the record is ambiguous — the length of the Gleissberg cycle, the size of Cycle 25's eventual maximum — that is said rather than resolved.
