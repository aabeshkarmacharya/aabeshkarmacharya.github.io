Seven public datasets, taken seriously

Public archives, downloaded raw and analysed from scratch: every recorded earthquake, every known atomic nucleus, every sunspot count since 1749, everything humans have left in orbit — and ten million keystrokes of English, to settle what a keyboard layout is actually worth.

The first four are interactive essays; the last is a tool you can point at your own writing. Every number was computed from the downloaded files rather than quoted, and where the data could not settle a question, they say so.

What these have in common

Each essay starts from a public archive that anyone can download, and computes everything on the page from it. That constraint turns out to matter more than the subject, because the same problem appears in all of them:

  • The seismic catalogue records twice as many earthquakes as it did in 1976. The Earth did not change; the network did.
  • “Stable” nuclides become unstable when instruments improve. Bismuth crossed that line in 2003.
  • The sunspot record was recalibrated end to end in 2015, moving historical values by roughly 45 per cent.
  • The satellite catalogue stores each object's most recent orbit, which quietly ruins any analysis of how long things last at a given altitude.

In every case the archive is partly a record of the world and partly a record of the people measuring it, and the interesting work is separating the two. Where that could not be done — whether great earthquakes have genuinely become more frequent, for instance — the essays say so rather than picking a side.
