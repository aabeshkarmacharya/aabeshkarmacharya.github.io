Four Million Rupees an Aana

Every plot of land for sale in the Kathmandu Valley carries a price, a road width and a tole name — but no coordinates. Join them to the map and a surface appears: what the ground costs, anywhere in the valley, and which of its features you are actually paying for.

An aana is 342 square feet — a little under a fifth of a tennis court. The median plot advertised in the Kathmandu Valley asks Rs 4,300,000 for one. A modest four-aana plot, the commonest size on the market, is therefore a seventeen-million-rupee proposition before anyone has laid a brick.

That median hides an enormous range. In Jhamsikhel, land asks thirteen million rupees an aana. Eleven kilometres south in Chapagaun, the same aana asks one and a half million. The valley is not one land market but a steep gradient, and almost everything about it can be predicted from three things: how far you are from the centre, how wide the road to your gate is, and what that road is made of.

Method

What this is, and what it is not

Every listing on the Nepali property marketplace nepalhomes.com was downloaded — 7,676 of them — and filtered to the 2,206 plots of bare land offered for sale in Kathmandu, Lalitpur or Bhaktapur. Of those, 2,115 carried a price, a parseable area and a position.

The listings name a tole but give no coordinates, so each of the 432 distinct locality names was geocoded once against OpenStreetMap. Distances, road classes, rivers, schools, hospitals and shops all come from OpenStreetMap; elevation from a digital terrain model. The model itself is a ridge regression on the logarithm of price per aana, with a spatial correction fitted to its own residuals.

Three things worth knowing before you trust a number

These are asking prices. Nepal records transaction prices at the Land Revenue Office and does not publish them. Everything here is what sellers hope for, which sits above what plots actually change hands for, and the gap is not uniform across the valley.

A tole is not a plot. Position is the centroid of the locality, so two plots a kilometre apart in the same tole share one set of coordinates. That puts a floor under the error no amount of modelling can lift, and it is most of why a quarter of localities miss by more than 25%.

Coverage follows the agents, not the land. South Lalitpur is thick with listings; large parts of the rim have none. The blank areas of the map are blank on purpose.

One coefficient that is not what it looks like

Distance from the Ring Road comes out positive — land further from it appears dearer. That is not a finding about the Ring Road; it is collinearity with distance from the centre. Once centrality is accounted for, what is left is that the expensive newer developments — Bhaisepati, Budhanilkantha — sit outside the ring while the dense old city sits inside it. Read the two coefficients together or not at all.
