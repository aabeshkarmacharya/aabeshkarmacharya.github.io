What the archive cannot see

Almost everything that decides the outcome. The database records which peak, which season, member or hired, oxygen or not, age and sex. It does not record how fit you are, how many seasons you have climbed, how good your guides are, how heavy your pack is, or — above all — what the weather does in your particular summit window. Those are most of the answer. What is fitted here is the base rate for a category of person, which is a useful thing to know and is not a prediction about you.

The death model is weak, and says so. It separates who died from who lived with a held-out AUC of —, where 0.5 is a coin and 1.0 is perfect. The summit model reaches —. That gap is the honest finding of this page: the record can tell you fairly well who gets to the top and barely at all who comes home. Dying on a mountain is mostly bad luck arriving at a specific place and minute, and no table of categories will predict it.

Deaths arrive in clusters. An avalanche kills whoever is beneath it, so the cross-validation holds out whole expeditions rather than individual people. A split that put two victims of one serac on both sides would have flattered every score above.

The record thins out behind you. The fit starts at 2000 because before then the recording of hired staff is too uneven to compare, which is the neighbouring essay's own finding. It ends at —, where this copy of the database ends.
