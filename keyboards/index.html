QWERTY puts about a third of your keystrokes on the home row. On ordinary prose, every layout designed since has put at least three fifths there. This page measures what that difference is actually worth, on 10.5 million keystrokes of real text — and lets you rearrange the keys yourself to see if you can do better.

What is being counted

Six numbers that decide a layout

Nearly every argument about keyboard layouts is an argument about one of these, and most of them can be settled by counting. The corpus is fixed; only the arrangement changes.

Same-finger bigrams are the one that matters most. When two letters in a row fall on the same finger, that finger has to lift, move and press again while the rest of the hand waits. QWERTY does this on of letter pairs in ordinary prose — think of ed, de, un, ce. The best modern layouts are near .

Travel is how far the fingertips actually move, in millimetres, per thousand characters. It follows from the geometry rather than from taste: a real ANSI board is row-staggered, so the home row sits a quarter of a key to the right of the top row and the bottom row a half key right of that. Ignore the stagger and every distance is wrong.

Alternation and rolls pull against each other, which is why layout designers disagree. Alternating hands lets one hand travel while the other presses. Rolls — consecutive keys on the same hand moving inward toward the index finger — are faster still when they come off cleanly. Dvorak maximises alternation; Colemak accepts less of it to buy more rolls. Neither is simply right.

Lateral stretches and scissors are the awkward ones. A stretch is the index finger leaving its column to grab the middle of the board. A scissor is two rows spanned by neighbouring fingers with the short finger reaching up and the long one curled under. Getting scissors right requires knowing that the middle finger is the longest, which is why QWERTY's in is comfortable and its ct is not, despite being geometrically identical.

Searching

What falls out of distance alone

The Find a shorter layout button runs simulated annealing over the keys, the same procedure that produced Halmak. Its objective is fingertip travel and nothing else — travel is the only quantity here carrying a physical unit, and it already contains the cost of same-finger pairs, because a finger forced between two keys travels the distance between them. It is told nothing about rolls, alternation, or what a hand enjoys.

On literary English it reliably lands near 7.85 m per thousand characters, against QWERTY's 17.08, and drops same-finger pairs to about 1.0% — under Colemak's 1.52%, which is not a claim that it is a better layout, only that distance is most of what the metric rewards.

The interesting part is what it keeps rediscovering. Run it repeatedly and the objective settles within a few hundredths of a metre every time while the arrangement comes out different, so the surface has many near-equal minima rather than one answer. But the home row is nearly the same each run, and usually shares nine of its eleven letters with Colemak's. Nobody told it about Colemak. Two people and a search procedure, given the same language and the same hand, keep putting e a i o n s t r under the fingers — which is a reasonable amount of evidence that the layouts designed by hand were not arbitrary.

Method

How these numbers were made

The corpora. Literary English is six public-domain novels from Project Gutenberg — Austen, Melville, Carroll, Doyle, Dickens and Darwin — chosen for a spread of registers, totalling keystrokes. Source code is files sampled across the Python standard library, keystrokes. Only frequency counts are published, never the text.

A check worth doing. The prose corpus reproduces the published relative frequencies of English letters to within 2.4 percentage points summed across all twenty-six — e at 12.66% against a reference 12.70%, t at 9.06% against 9.06%. That agreement is the reason there is no separate “modern English” corpus here: at the level of letters and letter pairs, Victorian novels and contemporary prose are very nearly the same language. I tried to build one from Wikipedia anyway and stopped when the API rate-limited a sample down to nine articles, which is not a corpus and would have been worse than honest omission.

The geometry. A standard ANSI row-staggered board at 19.05 mm pitch. Row offsets are derived from the real widths of Tab (1.5u), Caps Lock (1.75u) and left Shift (2.25u). Fingers are assigned by touch-typing convention, with the index fingers covering two columns each.

Travel model. Each finger holds position rather than springing back to home after every press, because that is what fingers do. The reported figure combines the distance from each finger's home key to the key being struck with the extra movement forced by same-finger pairs. It is a lower bound on real movement, not a simulation of a hand.

What is folded. Case is folded and shift is accounted separately: no layout here changes which letters are capitalised. That the shift key is pressed on 3.1% of prose characters and 21.1% of code characters is itself a result, and a reason to be suspicious of any layout tuned only on prose. Bigrams never straddle a space, since a pair separated by a word break is not a pair anyone rolls.

An asymmetry worth naming. Layouts disagree about which punctuation belongs in the three letter rows at all. Dvorak puts - and = there; QWERTY leaves them on the number row, which this model does not include. So on text containing those characters the layouts are not being scored on quite the same keystrokes, and the readout says what fraction of your text it could place rather than passing over the difference.

Two implementations. Every metric was written twice — once in Python against the raw corpora, once in JavaScript for this page — and the two agree to three decimal places on all seven layouts and both corpora. The page checks this on load and says so below rather than asking to be trusted.

What this does not measure. Comfort, learning cost, and the fact that you already know QWERTY. Typing speed is not distance; the correlation between these metrics and words per minute is real but loose, and a practised QWERTY typist will beat their own first month on any layout here. Treat the numbers as describing the arrangement, not you.
