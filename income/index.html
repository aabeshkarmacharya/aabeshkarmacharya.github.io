Nine Thousand Rupees a Month

That is the median Nepali’s share of what their household spends — the middle of a country of 29.7 million. This is what money looks like across that whole population, percentile by percentile, and where an actual salary lands on it.

The curve

Everyone, lined up poorest to richest

Line up all 29.7 million Nepalis by how much their household spends per head, and read off the person standing at each percentile. The line below is that reading. It is steep at both ends and flat through the middle, which is the shape of a country where almost everyone is close to everyone else and a thin tail is not.

The vertical scale is logarithmic, because the top of an income distribution is always several times the bottom and a linear axis would crush the bottom 90 % into the floor.

The shape

The same survey, read the other way

The curve above answers “what does the person at rank p have?” Turn the question around — “how many people are at this many rupees?” — and the same numbers make the shape below: a steep climb, a crowded peak in the single-digit thousands, and a tail that thins out long before it reaches anything anyone would call a good salary.

This is the picture that makes the country legible. Nepal is not a place with rich people and poor people in two camps. It is a place where an enormous number of people are within a few thousand rupees of each other.

The ladder

Where a salary actually lands

The curve above measures consumption per person. A salary is earned by one person and feeds a household — four people, on the NLSS-IV’s own average. So to put a wage on the same axis, divide it by four and ask which percentile that household member occupies. Every wage below has been restated at 2022–23 prices first, so it is being compared against the curve at the same price level.

What this is, and is not

The word “salary” is doing a lot of work

Nepal does not publish a salary distribution. No agency releases a table of what the 10th, 50th or 90th-percentile earner takes home. What the country does publish, and what this page is built from, is the distribution of household consumption per person from the Nepal Living Standards Survey. The two are related but they are not the same quantity, and three gaps between them matter.

Most working Nepalis have no salary at all. Wage and salaried employees are  % of employment. The rest are self-employed, in family farming, or in own-account work. A salary distribution, even a perfect one, would describe a fifth of the workers.

A quarter of the money arrives from abroad. Remittances are  % of GDP — one of the highest shares in the world. That income is in the consumption curve, because the household spends it. It is in no wage table anywhere, because it was not earned in Nepal.

Surveys miss the top. Household surveys everywhere undercount the richest, who are hard to sample and understate their income. The p99 here should be read as a floor, not a measurement, and there is no reliable Nepali estimate of the top 1 % at all.

And the wage figure is eight years old — not for want of looking. The last completed Labour Force Survey was 2017/18; the fourth round is in the field as this is written, running to March 2027. When it reports it will be the first Nepali wage data in nearly a decade, and the first chance to put a real earnings distribution beside this consumption one. The living standards survey underneath the curve is 2022/23, which is the newest that exists: there is no NLSS-V yet.

What the curve is very good at is the other 99 %. Its shape, its middle, and its movement since 1995 are measured on 9,600 households with a published methodology, and the reconstruction below reproduces the official poverty rate to within a fortieth of a percentage point.
