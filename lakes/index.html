Two Hundred and Eighty Lakes

Across the Koshi, Gandaki and Karnali basins, satellites have mapped glacial lakes. Over eight years of imagery, of them grew by an amount larger than the error in measuring them. The rest either held still or could not be measured at all. This page counts them and locates them. It does not tell you what would happen if one emptied.

Read this first

“Potentially dangerous” is a judgement somebody made, not a thing anybody measured

The figure usually quoted for Nepal — a few dozen dangerous glacial lakes out of thousands — is the output of a ranking exercise. A team picks criteria: a minimum area, a dam made of moraine rather than rock, a glacier close enough to calve into the water, a slope above steep enough to fail. Then they apply them to satellite imagery and a list comes out the other end.

Every step of that is a decision. Move the minimum area and the list changes length. Change how close a glacier has to be and it changes membership. None of those thresholds is written into the mountain; they are written into the method, and different teams write them differently. A danger classification is an argument, and it should be read as one.

This inventory does not carry such a classification, and this page does not invent one. What it carries instead is a measurement: for each lake with enough clear years of imagery, a fitted rate of area change with an uncertainty attached, and a flag for whether the rate can be told apart from no change at all. That is a smaller claim. It is also one you can check.

What is up there

Mostly ponds, very high up

The word “lake” does a lot of work in this archive. The median one covers hectares — about two football pitches — and of them are smaller than a single hectare. Only of the reach ten hectares.

They sit high. Half are above metres, and the middle ninety per cent fall between and . That is above almost everything: above the last village, above the treeline, above the highest permanent settlement in these valleys. The whole inventory adds up to square kilometres of water, which is a small pond by the standards of any lowland lake district and is not the point. The point is where it is and what is below it.

The growing ones are, unsurprisingly, the bigger ones. They are of the lakes by count but hold of all the water surface in the inventory, and of the are glacier-fed. The median growing lake gained square kilometres a year, which is about a tenth of a hectare — small, steady, and compounding.

One thing the map makes plain that a basin table does not: a large share of the lakes in Nepal’s river basins are not in Nepal. of the sit north of the border, in the Tibetan headwaters, and of the growing lakes are there. Water does not stop at a border and neither does a flood, but monitoring, warning systems and evacuation planning all do.

A straight line is not a flood path. These distances are great-circle distances from the lake to the nearest named place. Water runs down a valley, not across a ridge, so the real distance along the channel is longer — sometimes much longer — and the nearest settlement in kilometres is often not the one downstream. The number measures proximity, not exposure. Working out which places are actually below which lake needs a river network and a drainage model, and neither is in this archive.

What this page will not do

Counting is what this archive can honestly support

There is an obvious next question and this page does not answer it. It does not estimate how much water is in any of these lakes, how fast one would empty, how long the flood would take to reach a village, how deep it would be when it arrived, or how many people it would find there.

Those numbers need things that are not here. Volume needs a bathymetric survey or a depth model calibrated against one; almost none of these lakes has ever been sounded. A breach needs to know what the dam is made of, how wide the crest is, how much ice is buried in it, and what would set it off. Routing the water needs a channel survey and a roughness estimate down the whole valley. Exposure needs a building footprint layer and somebody in it at the time of day the flood arrives.

Each of those could be guessed. Guessing all of them in sequence produces an arrival time in minutes and a depth in metres, printed to one decimal place, that no one reading it can see is wrong. That is worse than no number, because a wrong flood model is acted on. So the page stops at the edge of the archive: this many lakes, this big, this high, here, and this many of them getting bigger.

Every limitation, in one place

The danger classification is not here and is not derived. This inventory records area, elevation, basin, connectivity and a rate of change. Nothing in it ranks a lake as dangerous, and this page does not construct such a ranking from the columns that are present.

Growth is not danger. A lake can grow steadily for decades and never fail; a stable lake can be destroyed by an avalanche into it next spring. The imagery cannot see the ice above the water or the state of the moraine holding it.

The classification is automated. The outlines come from a neural network run over annual image mosaics. Validated against hand-drawn outlines it scores an F1 of for Sentinel-2, from a sample of outlines across lakes. Against two other published inventories it agrees at to . Good, not perfect, and the disagreement is not random: small lakes, frozen lakes and lakes on top of glaciers are where it goes wrong.

Area varies within a year as well as between years. Two images of the same lake taken in different months differ because the water level differs. Some of the fitted change is that, not trend, which is why the uncertainty and the significance flag exist and why this page uses them rather than the raw rate.

The window is eight years. to is short for a glacier. A lake that appears stable here may have doubled in the thirty years before the record starts.

Elevations are modelled, not surveyed. They come from a 30-metre global surface model with an error of about five metres, and in steep terrain rather more.
