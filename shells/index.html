What else is at my altitude?

Two shells of low orbit are more crowded than any other, and they are crowded with opposite things. One is full of working satellites belonging mostly to a single company. The other is full of fragments that nothing is going to clear, because the air that sweeps the low shells clean does not reach that high. Pick an altitude and this says which kind of neighbourhood you are moving into.

What this catalogue quietly will not tell you

It cannot tell you how long an orbit lasts. That is the obvious thing to ask of an archive holding both a launch date and a decay date, and it is unanswerable here: the apogee and perigee fields hold the last known orbit, so for anything that has re-entered they describe the orbit it died in. The median perigee of a decayed object in this file is about 200 kilometres, which is not a fact about lifetimes but a restatement of what re-entry is. The launch orbit has been overwritten. So no lifetime figure appears anywhere on this page.

Tracked is not the same as present. These are the objects large enough to be catalogued, roughly ten centimetres and up. The population of fragments too small to track and large enough to end a mission is estimated in the hundreds of thousands and appears nowhere in this file. Every count here is a lower bound of a different kind from the last page's.

A shared shell is not a close approach. Two objects at the same altitude and inclination may never come near each other; two crossing at different inclinations may miss by kilometres and still matter. Counting the band is a measure of how busy a neighbourhood is, not of what will pass your window.

It is a snapshot, and the busy shells are moving. The single largest fleet here grew from nothing in under a decade. A census taken today describes an orbit that will be materially more crowded across a normal mission life.
