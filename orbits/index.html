The Shells Above Us

Every tracked object in Earth orbit, placed by how high it flies and how steeply it crosses the equator. 34,384 of them. Space turns out to have architecture.

CelesTrak SATCAT · 5 September 2026  
The catalogue

Seventy thousand things, and where half of them went

The United States has catalogued every object it can track in Earth orbit since Sputnik. The list now runs to 70,580 entries. Slightly more than half of them have already come down.

What remains is 34,720 objects, and only 19,847 of those are working or defunct spacecraft. The rest — 12,523 pieces of debris and 2,295 spent rocket bodies — is litter that happens to be moving at eight kilometres a second.

The chart above is the whole population at once. Two things about it are worth saying before anything else. The vertical stripes are inclinations that a great many objects share, because a handful of orbits are far more useful than the rest. The horizontal stripes are altitudes, and one of them is almost entirely rubbish.

Vertical stripes

There are only a few orbits worth having

Inclination is the angle between an orbit and the equator, and in principle it can be anything. In practice the catalogue clusters hard, because a small number of angles buy you something specific.

The largest cluster is near 98 degrees — slightly retrograde, tilted past the pole. At that angle the Earth's equatorial bulge drags the orbit's plane around by almost exactly one degree a day, which is the rate the Earth goes round the sun. The orbit therefore keeps a fixed angle to the sun and crosses any given latitude at the same local time on every pass. Every imaging satellite that wants consistent shadows lives there. A quarter of everything on orbit is in that one band.

The spike at zero degrees is geostationary orbit: circle the equator once a day at 35,786 kilometres and you hover over a fixed spot on the ground. It is the only orbit in the sky that a dish on a roof can point at and forget. Everything in it is packed into a ring one Earth-diameter wide and, by international agreement, spaced by longitude.

Horizontal stripes

The junkyard at eight hundred kilometres

Sort the same population by altitude instead and a different structure appears. Below 500 kilometres the catalogue is almost entirely working spacecraft. Between 700 and 900 it is almost entirely garbage.

The cause is air. There is still atmosphere at 400 kilometres — not much, but enough that anything up there is continuously slowed and will re-enter within years whether anyone plans for it or not. By 800 kilometres the drag has fallen away to almost nothing, and an object put there stays for centuries.

Low orbit cleans itself. High orbit remembers everything.

That is why the busiest shell in the sky is also the cleanest, and why a band nobody much uses any more is the dirtiest.

A controlled experiment

Two missiles, nineteen years apart

Twice in this century a country has destroyed one of its own satellites with a missile to demonstrate that it could. The two events produced comparable amounts of wreckage. What happened to that wreckage afterwards was not comparable at all, and the catalogue records both outcomes precisely.

China destroyed the Fengyun-1C weather satellite in January 2007. It was in a sun-synchronous orbit at 791 by 811 kilometres. The catalogue picked up 3,531 trackable fragments, and 2,315 of them — 66 per cent — are still up there nineteen years later, at a median perigee of 764 kilometres. That single decision remains the largest contributor to trackable debris in low Earth orbit.

Russia destroyed Cosmos 1408 in November 2021. The catalogue picked up 1,806 fragments. Four are left. Not four per cent — four objects, 0.2 per cent, five years later. The test was conducted low enough that the atmosphere disposed of the evidence.

What the data can and cannot say here. The catalogue records each object's most recent orbit, not the orbit it started in, so it cannot be used to reconstruct the altitude of either detonation. What it does record without ambiguity is the outcome: 3,531 fragments with two thirds still flying, against 1,806 fragments with four. The surviving Fengyun-1C debris sits at a median perigee of 764 km. That is the whole explanation.

Between the two, in February 2009, the derelict Cosmos 2251 ran into the working Iridium 33 satellite at 789 kilometres — the first accidental collision between two intact satellites. It produced 2,370 catalogued fragments, of which 731 are still in orbit. Nobody chose that one.

The new arrivals

One company, a third of everything

The catalogue lists 12,811 objects named Starlink. 11,091 of them are in orbit right now. That is 31.9 per cent of every tracked object above the Earth, and 55.9 per cent of all catalogued payloads. The first one launched in 2019.

It would be easy to read that as the debris problem getting worse, and for the long term it may well be. But the altitude chart says something more specific. Starlink flies at about 550 kilometres, in the band that is 1 to 12 per cent debris — the part of the sky where the atmosphere still works. A failed Starlink satellite re-enters on its own within about five years whether anyone intervenes or not.

The 1,720 Starlink objects that have already decayed, out of 12,811 launched in seven years, are the visible evidence of that. It is a genuinely different situation from leaving 3,531 fragments at 800 kilometres, and the two should not be added together without saying so.

Which is not an argument that it is fine. Nothing in this catalogue speaks to collision probability, conjunction rates, the load on ground operators, or what happens to astronomy. It counts objects and records where they are. On that narrow question: the objects are increasing very fast, and they are mostly being put somewhere that empties itself.
Ownership

Who is up there

The catalogue attributes each object to the state or organisation responsible for it. The distribution is lopsided, and has become more so.

Taken together, the picture the catalogue draws is of a volume of space that is highly structured, unevenly used, and unevenly cleaned. There is a busy floor that sweeps itself, a quiet attic full of fragments that will outlast everyone reading this, and a thin ring at geostationary altitude that nothing will ever remove.

Method & sources

All data from the CelesTrak SATCAT (celestrak.org/pub/satcat.csv), retrieved 5 September 2026: 70,580 catalogued objects with object type, owner, launch and decay dates, orbital period, inclination, apogee and perigee. CelesTrak republishes the public catalogue maintained by the United States Space Force's 18th Space Defense Squadron.

Charts use the 34,384 objects that are Earth-orbiting, undecayed, and carry a usable inclination, apogee and perigee. Altitude throughout is the mean of apogee and perigee, both of which the catalogue gives as height above the Earth's surface rather than radius. Objects with apogees beyond 65,536 km are clamped for the plot and excluded from no statistic.

One thing this dataset cannot do. The orbital elements are each object's most recent values, not its values at launch. Any analysis of the form “how long does something last at altitude X” is therefore circular — everything decays through low altitude, so every decayed object appears to have been low. That analysis was attempted here, produced a result that was non-monotonic and obviously wrong, and was discarded. The altitude claims on this page all concern objects currently in orbit, where the recorded elements are the real ones.

Only trackable objects are in this catalogue. Estimates of the debris population below roughly 10 cm run to the hundreds of millions and come from models and radar sampling, not from tracking. Nothing on this page counts them.
