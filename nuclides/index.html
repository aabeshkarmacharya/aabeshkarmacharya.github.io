The Valley of Stability

Every combination of protons and neutrons that has ever been observed to hold together, even for 86 yoctoseconds. There are 3,383 of them. Two hundred and forty-four last forever.

The chart

Almost nothing is allowed to exist

Protons and neutrons can in principle be combined in any numbers you like. In practice the overwhelming majority of those combinations fly apart before they can be called a nucleus. What survives is a narrow diagonal streak through an otherwise empty plane, and everything in chemistry, geology and biology happens inside it.

The chart above plots neutron count across and proton count up. Each square is one nuclide that has actually been made and measured. The empty region in the upper left is not unexplored territory — it is territory where a nucleus would shed protons faster than it could be assembled. The empty region below right is the same story for neutrons.

Pairing

Nuclei would rather be even

Sort the 244 stable nuclides by whether their proton and neutron counts are even or odd and the result is not close. 140 have both even. Only four have both odd.

The four exceptions are worth naming, because between them they are a surprisingly large fraction of the universe you interact with: deuterium, lithium-6, boron-10 and nitrogen-14. The last is 99.6 per cent of the nitrogen in the atmosphere, which is to say most of the air.

Every other odd–odd combination that has ever been assembled — and hundreds have been — eventually rearranges itself into something even.

Shells

Seven numbers that decide everything

Electrons fill shells, and an atom with a full outer shell is chemically inert. Nucleons do the same thing, and the shell closures fall at 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82 and 126. Eugene Wigner reportedly called them magic because nobody could explain them; Maria Goeppert Mayer and Hans Jensen worked out why, and shared the 1963 Nobel Prize for it.

The evidence is sitting in the data. Tin has fifty protons, and tin has ten stable isotopes — more than any other element. Across the whole chart, elements with a magic proton number average 4.67 stable isotopes each; everything else averages 2.51.

Binding

The curve that runs the universe

Take every nuclide, work out how much energy holds it together, divide by the number of nucleons, and plot it against mass number. The resulting curve rises steeply from hydrogen, flattens into a broad maximum around mass 60, and declines gently all the way to uranium.

Everything about nuclear energy is contained in that shape. Below the peak, combining nuclei moves you uphill and releases energy: that is fusion, and it is why stars shine. Above the peak, splitting nuclei moves you uphill and releases energy: that is fission, and it is why reactors work. The peak itself is where both stop paying.

Is the peak iron or nickel?

Both, depending on what you ask. The highest binding energy per nucleon in the whole chart belongs to nickel-62, at 8.7946 MeV. The lowest mass per nucleon belongs to iron-56. Those are different questions: binding energy asks how hard the nucleus is to pull apart, mass per nucleon asks how much matter each nucleon actually carries — and since a neutron is heavier than a proton, a neutron-rich nucleus can be more tightly bound and still weigh more.

Stars care about the mass, because photodisintegration equilibrium in a stellar core settles wherever mass per nucleon is lowest. So the ash at the centre of a dying massive star is iron, and the textbook is right to call it the iron peak, even though nickel-62 wins the binding-energy contest by two parts in ten thousand.

Both numbers are in this dataset, which is the only reason the question is settleable here rather than by assertion. Ni-62: 8.7946 MeV per nucleon against Fe-56's 8.7904. Fe-56: 998838.1 micro-atomic-mass-units per nucleon against Ni-62's 998844.3. Neither rounds away.
Time

From 86 yoctoseconds to 1025 years

Half-lives in this catalogue span fifty-four orders of magnitude. It is difficult to convey how large that range is, so here are the ends of it.

The shortest belongs to hydrogen-5: two neutrons bolted onto a proton, gone in 8.6 × 10−23 seconds. In that time light travels about a quarter of the width of the nucleus itself. Calling it a nucleus is generous; it is closer to a resonance that briefly resembles one.

The longest belongs to tellurium-128, which decays by emitting two electrons at once and has a measured half-life of 7.7 × 1024 years. That is roughly 560 trillion times the present age of the universe. A kilogram of it would lose a few dozen atoms while you read this sentence, and that is enough to measure.

Two missing rungs

Why there is any carbon at all

Five mass numbers have no stable nuclide anywhere on the chart: 5, 8, 147, 151 and 183. The first two are the reason the universe is not simply hydrogen and helium.

Big Bang nucleosynthesis builds up by adding nucleons one at a time. It reaches helium-4 easily. The next step would be mass 5, and mass 5 does not exist: helium-5 falls apart in 7 × 10−22 seconds, lithium-5 in 3.7 × 10−22. The alternative is to fuse two helium-4 nuclei and make mass 8, and mass 8 does not exist either: beryllium-8 survives 8.2 × 10−17 seconds and splits back into two alphas.

The ladder out of helium is missing two rungs, and the early universe could not climb past them.

Stars get past it by cheating. In a hot enough core, beryllium-8 forms and dissociates constantly, and a tiny equilibrium population of it exists at any instant. If a third helium-4 arrives during that 10−16-second window, carbon-12 results. The reaction is far too slow at first glance to make the carbon we observe — which is what led Fred Hoyle, in 1953, to predict that carbon-12 must have an excited state at just the right energy to speed it up. It does. It was found within a year, and it is the reason you are made of anything.

Reclassification

The element that stopped being stable

For most of the twentieth century, bismuth-209 was the heaviest stable nuclide known. It is the only isotope of bismuth — 100 per cent of all natural bismuth — and it sat at the top of the stable list as the last thing before the radioactive elements begin.

In 2003 a group at the Institut d'Astrophysique Spatiale in Orsay measured its alpha decay. The half-life is 2.01 × 1019 years: about 1.5 billion times the age of the universe. Bismuth is radioactive. It was simply too slow to notice.

What that does to the count. This dataset lists 244 stable nuclides. Older references say 254, or 256, or 264. The differences are not errors — they are the same measurement problem. “Stable” in practice means “no decay has been detected yet,” and every improvement in detection moves a few nuclides across the line. The number is a statement about instruments as much as about nuclei.

Nothing in the world changed when bismuth was reclassified. Work the activity out from the half-life and a gram of bismuth emits about 99 alpha particles a year — one every three and a half days, from a sample containing 2.9 × 1021 atoms. But a category that everyone had treated as a property of matter turned out to be a property of how hard anyone had looked, which is a thing worth knowing about categories.

Chains

Four ladders down

Alpha decay removes two protons and two neutrons; beta decay converts a neutron into a proton. Neither changes mass number by anything other than four or zero. So a heavy nucleus falling apart can only ever reach mass numbers congruent to its own, modulo four — and that divides the entire heavy end of the chart into exactly four separate descent routes that never intersect.

Nothing below is looked up. Take a starting nuclide, read its dominant decay mode from the table, apply the arithmetic, look up where you land, repeat until you reach something stable. The four classical decay series fall out of that loop.

Three of the four end on lead: mass 208, 206 and 207. The fourth does not, and the reason is the bismuth measurement from the previous section.

The neptunium series was supposed to stop at bismuth-209. Since 2003 it has one more rung.

Bismuth-209 sits in the 4n+1 family. As long as it counted as stable, that series ended there — and that is what the textbooks said for seventy years. Once its alpha decay was measured, the chain acquired one more step, down to thallium-205, which really is stable. A half-life of twenty billion billion years is not a detail for anyone waiting on the outcome, but it changed where a diagram ends.

Why only three of these are still around. The parent half-lives are 1.4 × 1010 years for thorium-232, 4.5 × 109 for uranium-238 and 7.0 × 108 for uranium-235 — all comparable to the age of the Earth, so all three are still with us. Neptunium-237 lasts only 2.1 × 106 years. Whatever the Earth formed with ran out long ago, and the 4n+1 series exists on Earth today only where someone has made it.
Discovery

A century of filling in the chart

Almost every nuclide on this chart was discovered by somebody, in a year, using a machine. The record goes back to the 1900s and has not stopped.

The chart is not finished. Models of the nuclear force predict several thousand more bound nuclides on the neutron-rich side, in a region no accelerator has reached. Whether the shell closures that make 50 and 82 magic near stability still hold out there is an open question, and it matters well beyond nuclear physics: the rapid neutron-capture process that builds gold and uranium inside merging neutron stars runs straight through that unmapped territory.

Everything in the chart above, in other words, is the part we have been able to make.

Method & sources

All nuclear data from the IAEA Nuclear Data Services live API (nds.iaea.org/relnsd), ground_states table, retrieved 5 September 2026: 3,383 nuclides with Z ≥ 1, carrying half-life, decay modes and branching ratios, binding energy per nucleon, atomic mass, natural abundance, spin-parity and first-observation year. The underlying evaluation is ENSDF, maintained by the international Nuclear Structure and Decay Data network.

“Stable” throughout means the value this dataset records, not a claim about physics: 244 nuclides for which no decay has been observed. Nuclides are grouped by their dominant decay mode — β− includes double beta decay and beta-delayed emission, β+/EC includes electron capture and double electron capture, and the remaining group holds proton and neutron emission, spontaneous fission and isomeric transitions. Every nuclide's exact recorded mode is in its tooltip.

Binding energies are as tabulated (keV per nucleon, converted to MeV); the mass-per-nucleon comparison uses the tabulated atomic masses in micro-atomic-mass-units divided by mass number. Half-life histogram bins are two decades wide. Of the 3,139 unstable nuclides, 2,961 carry a measured half-life; the remaining 178 are omitted from time-based figures and still appear on the chart under their decay mode.

Every figure and every number in the text is computed from that single download. Where a claim needed checking — whether the binding-energy peak is iron or nickel, whether technetium and promethium really are the only gaps below bismuth — it was checked against the data rather than recalled.
