The Rice We Grow and Still Buy

In 1961 a hectare of Nepali paddy yielded more than a hectare of Indian, Bangladeshi or South Asian paddy. It has yielded less than all three for most of the years since. The ground was lost early, and it has been closing since 1990. The imports have not.

Read this first

Three reasons to hold these numbers loosely

FAOSTAT is a reporting system, not a measuring instrument. Almost everything below is a number Nepal sent to Rome, and the way Nepal arrived at it changed over the period the chart covers.

Limitation

1. The numbers are Nepal’s own, from a survey whose method moved

FAO does not go and weigh the harvest. Nepal’s area, production and yield come from the national statistics office’s crop survey, and that survey’s sampling frame, crop-cutting protocol and district coverage were not the same in 1965, 1995 and 2020. A change in how a thing is counted looks exactly like a change in the thing. Nothing in the archive separates the two.

2. The country was rebuilt in 2015

Nepal’s constitution replaced 14 zones and 75 districts with 7 provinces and 753 local units. The enumeration areas the crop survey runs on were redrawn on top of that. A series that runs to under one label is therefore reporting two different administrative definitions of the same country, and the join is not marked anywhere in the data.

3. Some of these values are not reported at all

FAOSTAT flags every cell, and most charts of it drop the flag. Here is what the flags say. Of Nepal’s annual rice yields, are marked A, an official figure, and is marked E, an FAO estimate — the most recent year, which is also the highest. On the import side it is worse: of the supply-line values in Nepal’s food balance sheet carry I, imputed by FAO, and carry A. Not one figure in the import series on this page was reported by Nepal. They are FAO’s reconstruction of a balance that has to add up.

None of that makes the archive useless. It makes it a record of what was reported, which is a different object from a record of what was grown. The yield comparison below survives it reasonably well, because the methodological drift is small next to a factor of two. The import series survives it less well, and the page says so where it matters.

One arithmetic check the page does make. FAOSTAT publishes yield as its own column and also publishes the production and the area it was divided by. Recomputing yield from those two columns reproduces the published figure to kg per hectare across all four areas and all years, which is the rounding and nothing else. The build fails if it ever stops doing so.

Over the four years they overlap, every Nepali rice tonnage in the new series is times the old one — a change of commodity basis, milled against paddy equivalent, not a change in what was shipped. What this does not show

Five limits

The reporting is the measurement. Stated at the top and it does not go away: every Nepali number here came through a national crop survey whose method and whose country both changed during the period. Where the archive flags an estimate, this page says so; where it flags an imputation, this page says so. It cannot flag a methodology that drifted quietly.

Southern Asia contains Nepal. The regional aggregate is not an independent comparator. Nepal is about per cent of its rice area, so the contamination is small, but it is not zero and it flatters the comparison slightly in Nepal’s direction.

Harvested area is not land. A hectare cropped twice counts twice. Cropping intensity rose over this period, so part of what reads as more area is the same ground used harder — and yield per harvested hectare is not yield per hectare of ground.

No cause is established anywhere on this page. Irrigation, fertiliser, seed variety, land fragmentation, labour leaving for the Gulf: all of these are offered as explanations of Nepali yields and none of them is in this archive. The page reports what was harvested from how much land, and stops.

The two archives do not share a calendar. The crop domain and the food balance domain attribute the same harvest to different years, because one is a crop year and the other a supply year. Nothing here divides a number from one by a number from the other except the per-head series, which uses only population from the food balance and says so.
