One Hundred Forty-Four Destinations, Six Countries

Nepal issued labour permits for places last fiscal year. Six of them took four permits in every five. That is less concentrated than it used to be, and the six are not the six they were.

Before anything else

This archive cannot see India

It counts permits issued, not people who went, and not people who stayed. And because Nepali citizens may work in India without a permit, no permit is issued, no row is written, and the largest destination for Nepali workers does not appear in the only official record of where Nepali workers go. Every share on this page is a share of the permitted destinations alone. The denominator is wrong by an amount nobody here can measure.

That is not a small correction to apply afterwards. Nepal and India have kept an open border since 1950; a worker from Kailali crossing to Uttar Pradesh for the sugarcane season fills in nothing and is counted by nobody. The surveys that have tried to size that flow put it in the hundreds of thousands to millions, which would make India comparable to everything on this page put together or larger. This page does not use those estimates, because they are not in this archive and mixing a survey estimate into a permit count would produce a number with no meaning at all.

Three more things the record is not. A permit is a document, not a departure — some are issued and never used. A permit is not a person, because a worker who goes twice appears twice. And a permit expiring says nothing about whether the worker came home, so nothing here measures how many Nepalis are abroad right now. What it does measure, it measures completely: every destination, including the ones with a single permit in a year.

The archive

Every country, not the top ten

The Ministry of Labour publishes a table most statistical annexes do not bother with: the full list. Not the Gulf plus Malaysia plus “others”, but every destination a permit was issued for, down to the one permit for Burkina Faso.

Five fiscal years are available at that resolution, from 2019/20 to 2023/24 — Nepal’s fiscal year runs from mid-July, so 2023/24 is 2080/81 in the Bikram Sambat calendar. Across them the record holds permits: new ones and renewals for workers already abroad. Everything that follows counts new permits only, because a renewal tells you where somebody already is, not where anybody chose to go.

The finding

The concentration is falling

The expectation going in was that a handful of countries take almost everything and always have. The first half is true. The second is not.

The six largest destinations took per cent of new permits in and per cent in . That is a fall of percentage points across the fiscal years the record covers. The Herfindahl index — the sum of the squared shares, which is the standard way of asking how concentrated a market is — went the same way, from in 2019/20 to in , after peaking at in .

Eighty per cent to six destinations is still an extraordinary concentration. A worker choosing where to go is choosing between six places and a rounding error. But it is thirteen points less extraordinary than it was, and the direction has been consistent enough through a pandemic and a recovery to be worth taking seriously.

The first two years are a floor, not a measurement. For 2017/18 and 2018/19 the full annex does not exist and the published table names only fifteen destinations, with permits collapsed into “others”. The six largest of the named ones are used. If a destination hiding inside that residual were bigger than the sixth named one — , on permits — it would replace it and push the share up. So the real fall is at least as large as the one drawn here, and possibly larger.

The same thing shows up in the shape of the whole distribution rather than one number from it. Stack the destinations largest first and read off how much of the total you have after each one: the curve reaches the top later every year.

Who the six are

The sixth seat changed hands

A falling concentration would be a quiet story if the same six countries were simply losing a little each year. They are not. The membership of the top six has changed, and one of the six is now in Europe.

In the six were Malaysia and five Gulf states, and the sixth was . In the sixth is , on per cent — a country that issued almost no permits to Nepali workers before 2019/20. South Korea and Romania sit just below it. The Gulf has not collapsed; something else has been added beside it.

The individual series are violent. Malaysia took of new permits in 2019/20 and in 2020/21, when it closed to foreign workers. Then in 2022/23, when it reopened and the backlog went through at once — the largest share any destination has taken in these five years — and the year after. What this does not show

Four limits

India is absent, and it is probably the largest destination. Stated at the top and repeated here, because it is the one thing that could make every share on this page misleading. Nothing in this archive can be adjusted for it.

Five years is a short series, and three of them are a pandemic. 2020/21 has new permits against the following year. A trend fitted through that would be a trend fitted through a border closing and reopening. Nothing here is fitted; the years are shown as they are.

The two editions do not record the same routes. The 2022 report splits permits into individual, regularisation, recruitment agency and renewal; the 2024 report drops regularisation and adds government-to-government. The new-against-renewal split survives that change and reproduces both reports’ own totals exactly, which is why the page uses it and does not compare routes across the boundary.

The reports do not quite add up to themselves. Summing every row of each annex lands within five permits of the grand total the same report prints, out of three-quarters of a million. The difference is in the published tables, not in the parsing; analyze.py refuses to write a payload if it grows past ten.

Method. The annex tables are read out of the PDFs with pypdf and summed by destination. Destination names are reconciled between the two editions, which spell a dozen of them differently and misspell Cambodia. A permit counts as new if it is not a renewal. The parse is checked twice before the page is written: the per-year totals must land within ten permits of the totals the reports print for themselves, and the destinations named in Table 3.2 must match the annex row by row for the three fiscal years both cover — they agree exactly. assemble.py then recomputes the headline share from the per-destination counts in the payload and refuses to write a page that disagrees with itself. Everything is computed from the two reports by fetch.py and analyze.py; no figure is quoted from a secondary source.
