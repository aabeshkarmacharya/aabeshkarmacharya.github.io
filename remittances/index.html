Six Times What We Sell

In the best year Nepal’s exports have ever had, remittance still arrived at six times their value. There is no published series for this. It had to be rebuilt out of PDFs.

What is not published

There is no series

Nepal Rastra Bank publishes one macroeconomic bulletin a month. It is a typed PDF. There is no CSV, no API and no spreadsheet behind it, and the monthly numbers in it exist nowhere else in a form a machine can read.

The bank’s department sitemaps list English bulletins. of them come back as PDFs. of the URLs serve something that is not a PDF at all, and failed outright. What arrived is about characters of extracted text per issue, spanning to .

Of those documents, state a remittance total in rupees and state a merchandise export total as well. state neither. Those are not a parsing failure. Before 2011/12 the bulletins quote growth rates and print no levels, so there is nothing in them to extract; one further issue was published as tables with no prose at all.

Every bulletin reports the fiscal year to date, not the month. “During the eleven months of 2024/25, remittance inflows increased 15.5 percent to Rs.1532.93 billion” is eleven months of money, not one. A single month is the difference between two consecutive issues of the same fiscal year. Nepal’s fiscal year restarts in mid-July, so month one is not a difference at all, and there is no thirteenth month to subtract from. Get that wrong and every figure on this page is wrong by a factor of about ten.

Differencing is also why a gap in the archive costs two months rather than one: a month is lost whenever either of the issues it sits between is missing. cumulative observations across fiscal-month slots yield months. slots stay empty. The series runs to .

The largest hole is a whole fiscal year. The archive holds of the twelve bulletins should have, so that year contributes nothing at all. Of the fiscal years the archive touches, are complete; the rest are partial and are drawn as such.

Two of the are filed under the wrong period. NRB’s URL for the eleven-month 2019/20 issue says ten months, and the real ten-month issue sits at the same address with a -2 on the end. A document states its own period on nearly every page and the filename states it once, so where the two disagree the document wins — and both relabellings are counted rather than quietly applied.

What moves it

The multiple is an export statistic

Remittance barely moves. Exports move constantly. Almost every wobble in the ratio is the denominator.

Across the consecutive pairs of complete fiscal years in this record, remittance changed by between % and %. It went down in of the , in , by half a per cent, in the year the world shut its airports. Merchandise exports changed by between % and %, and went down in of them.

Both of the big export years are the same trade, and the bulletins name it themselves. Exports went from Rs billion in to Rs billion in , which took the multiple down to — its lowest since . The next year they fell % to Rs billion, and the bulletins of that year open their list of falling items with “soyabean oil, palm oil, oil cakes”. The trade returned in at Rs billion, and the list of rising items opens with soyabean oil again.

So the denominator of this ratio is, in two of the last five years, substantially a margin on imported oil sold on. The numerator is wages, earned by people who left.

Three shocks

You can find the earthquake without being told where it is

A reconstructed series is worth nothing if it cannot see things that certainly happened. This one sees all three.

April 2015. The earthquake struck on 25 April, inside the month running mid-April to mid-May. Remittance for that month came to Rs billion, against Rs billion the month before, and stayed at that level the month after. Exports fell from Rs billion to Rs billion. The multiple went from to .

The blockade, autumn 2015. Freight across the Indian border stopped in late September 2015 and did not properly restart until February. Monthly exports fell from Rs billion in to Rs billion in — down twenty-eight per cent in two months. Remittance over the same two months went from Rs billion to Rs billion, down ten per cent. The multiple went from to .

March 2020. Nepal closed on 24 March. Remittance for mid-March to mid-April came to Rs billion, against Rs billion a month earlier: it more than halved in four weeks. Exports in the following month fell to Rs billion, about a third of normal, and that month holds the record for the whole series — remittance at times exports.

Remittance recovered inside two months and exports did not, which is why the ratio’s peak is a month after its numerator’s trough.

Checking it

Two checks the archive pays for itself

A number reconstructed by subtraction should be tested against a number somebody typed. The bulletins supply both tests.

The direct one. Since 2023 many issues print the single review month on its own line as well as the cumulative total: “During mid-May to mid-June 2025 (Jestha month), remittance inflows stood at Rs. 176.32 billion”. That figure is never used to build the series. There are months where it can be held against the differenced value. of the agree to the last digit printed. The median error is % and the worst is %, which is NRB revising its own provisional figure between two issues.

The indirect one, covering the whole record. Every bulletin states its change against the same period a year earlier. Dividing the level by that change reconstructs the earlier level — and the earlier level sits in a different bulletin, parsed separately. There are places in the archive where the two can be compared. The median disagreement is %, are within one per cent, and the worst is %.

Neither check is a test of whether NRB is right. Both are tests of whether the parser read what NRB wrote, and of how much the bank moves a number after first printing it. The answer to the second is: by about a hundredth of a per cent in the median case, and by up to two per cent where the first print was provisional.

What this does not show

Five limits

These are first prints, not revisions. Each month is built from the issues published at the time, so a figure NRB later restated is carried here as it was first typed. That is deliberate — it keeps each month independent of what came after it — but it means the series will not match a table NRB publishes today for the same period.

Merchandise exports only. Services are excluded: no tourism, no transport, no the education payments that flow the other way. Adding services would lower the multiple, and NRB reports them on a different basis in a different paragraph.

Nominal rupees throughout. Nothing here is deflated and nothing is converted to dollars. The levels rise partly because Nepali prices rose. The ratio is unaffected, since both sides are in the same rupees of the same month, and the ratio is the argument.
